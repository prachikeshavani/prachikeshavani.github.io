## The eight hours that bothered me

When I joined Hyster-Yale as a marketing intelligence analyst in May
2025, I inherited a translation workflow that kept our global content
moving: websites, SEO content, product manuals, blog posts and brand
material, all translated into multiple languages.

The translations mattered. The workflow around them was the problem.
Three of us were spending around eight hours a week, collectively, on
pure administration. A requester would submit a form in Wrike (our
project management tool). An analyst would then manually create a
matching project in XTM (our translation management platform), upload
the source files, wait for XTM to analyse the project and generate
costs, review the costs, send purchase orders to the translation
agencies, wait for the translations, download the finished files, and
upload them back into the original Wrike task.

In between all of that, files travelled through Box. A large, years-old,
password-protected Excel workbook tracked everything, and only one
person could use it at a time.

Eight hours a week is a full working day. I kept thinking about what
three analysts could do with that time instead of moving files between
systems.

Within about three months of joining, I decided to fix it. I am not a
software engineer. Before this project, my Python experience was data
analysis: reading CSVs, finding patterns, building reports. I had never
touched an API. That turned out to be less of a blocker than I expected,
and more of the story than I planned for.

## Mapping before coding

My first step was not writing code. I mapped the entire process end to
end: where information was created, where it needed to go, which steps
were repeated identically every time, and where a human genuinely needed
to make a judgement call.

Then I explored the APIs of both systems to see what was technically
possible. This two-sided view mattered more than anything else in the
project, because it forced an important distinction: a step being
automatable does not mean it should be automated.

This process involved real money. The organisation spends roughly 2
million euros a year on translation. Individual projects typically cost
200 to 300 euros, but bigger ones run into the thousands, and anything
over 1,000 euros needed my manager to be aware before it went to an
agency. If I automated project creation carelessly, I would be
automating the commitment of company spend. That line shaped the whole
design.

## What I automated, and what I deliberately didn't

The scripts I built in Python connect Wrike and XTM through their APIs.
They transfer the request details and source files from a new Wrike task
into XTM, and later retrieve the translated files and return them to the
original task. The Wrike task ID becomes the XTM project name, so every
translation project traces back to its request automatically.

Everything repetitive and predictable became a script. Everything
involving judgement stayed with a person.

Three human checkpoints survived the automation:

1. **Before agency handoff.** Once XTM analyses a project, an analyst
   reviews the agency assignments and the costs before purchase orders
   go out. Large projects still get flagged to management. XTM already
   had purchase-order functionality, so I used that rather than building
   my own emailing layer.

2. **Before files come back.** A translation project can look finished
   while linguists still have open questions about the source content.
   Returning files too early creates rework. So the retrieval script
   only runs when an analyst enters the XTM project ID and triggers it.

3. **Before completion.** An analyst checks the returned files, confirms
   the language suffixes are correct on the filenames, and only then
   marks the Wrike task complete, which notifies everyone involved.

The principle was simple: automate the predictable administration,
protect the financial and content decisions. My manager is not technical
and none of my colleagues could read Python, so being able to explain
exactly where the humans stayed in control was as important as the
automation itself.

## Learning APIs the hard way

I learned REST APIs from scratch during this project: authentication,
headers, endpoints, pagination, reading documentation that assumed
knowledge I did not have yet.

The Wrike API documentation was clear. The older on-premises XTM
documentation was not well maintained, and their authentication setup
fought me for a while. I had the base URL and credentials, I could
generate a token, and my requests still came back rejected. I went back
and forth with XTM support trying to pin down what I was missing. I
eventually got it working by revisiting the authentication
configuration, with vendor support and the internal access approvals
falling into place. To this day I have not verified the exact root
cause, and I would rather say that honestly than invent a tidy
technical explanation.

There was a second wall: internal security. Company policy blocked
laptops from accessing external APIs, and other AI websites were blocked
by the firewall. So I did this properly. I explained the project and
its risks to our cybersecurity team and got approval for API access,
Python and VS Code. Then I used the one AI tool I was allowed, basic
Copilot, as a tutor: asking it to explain concepts and errors, then
testing everything against the real systems. I never worked outside the
security controls, and getting through that approval process taught me
how to talk about technical risk with non-technical decision makers.

A lot of this happened on Friday evenings and weekends, because the
automation was built alongside my actual job, not instead of it.

## Testing with lorem ipsum

I tested end to end, not just function by function. I created a lorem
ipsum document and pushed it through the entire live workflow.

Before the test purchase order went out, I told the receiving agency it
was a demo: it should not appear in their monthly reconciliation as a
real project. The automation carried my fake document into XTM, the
costs generated, and the demo PO landed on the agency's side, where
they confirmed the project and cost information were visible.

The costs were naturally tiny, because nobody was translating anything
real. That was fine. The point was to prove information flowed through
correctly.

This was manual, practical end-to-end testing with sample projects. I
did not build an automated test suite, and I am not going to pretend I
did. That is on my list for the next version, together with persistent
run state, idempotent retries instead of my "add _1, _2 suffixes to
suspected duplicates" convention, and status polling to replace the
fixed 500-second wait for XTM cost generation.

## The curveball: migrating to XTM Cloud

About six months after the automation went live, XTM retired its
on-premises version and we had to move to XTM Cloud. I expected this to
be a one-line change: update the API base URL.

It was not. The new cloud domain was blocked by our firewall, and
connections failed with an SSL handshake error. I traced it by
connecting through the company VPN, and the connection worked
immediately, which confirmed the firewall was the blocker. The VPN requirement stayed, but once
connectivity was sorted, the main workflows carried on working.

The lesson stuck with me: an integration never runs in isolation. It
lives inside someone else's network policies, vendor roadmaps and
migration schedules, and you have to design for the environment, not
just the code.

## The results

| Area | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Main translation admin | ~8 analyst-hours/week across 3 analysts | ~10 to 15 minutes, once each morning, for one analyst |
| Month-end technical-publication reporting | ~2 to 2.5 days for one analyst | ~2 hours |
| File and project movement | Manual downloads, uploads and re-entry | Handled by the scripts |
| Oversight | Manual checks throughout | Human approval kept at agency handoff, costs and final completion |

A quick honesty note on these numbers, because I care about that. The
eight hours was a weekly total across three people, taken from our time
records and confirmed by my manager. The 10 to 15 minutes is the daily
routine for one person. They are not a like-for-like percentage
calculation, and I will not present them as one.

Alongside the main workflow, I built a separate Python reporting script
for month-end technical publications. It pulls roughly 800 to 900
projects for a date range and exports the costs XTM has already
generated into a CSV. What used to take two and a half days of
extraction, manual totalling and re-keying totals into our old SAP
system now takes about two hours. The SAP step stays manual because
automating that legacy version was not practical.

What did this not do? It did not improve translation quality, SEO
rankings or revenue, and I have no evidence it did, so I will not claim
it. What it did do was give my team a working day back every week and
make month-end bearable. The analysts who were initially sceptical about
changing a process they had used for years became its biggest
advocates the moment they saw the time savings.

## What I took away from this

The hardest part of this project was never the Python. It was
understanding the real process, deciding which decisions belonged to
humans, getting through security approvals, and building something that
other non-technical people could actually operate and trust.

I also learned to separate a script that runs from a process that is
reliable. Logging that tells you which stage failed, version control in
Azure DevOps, credentials kept out of the code, honest testing, clear
ownership. All of that is part of the deliverable, not decoration.

I built this at Hyster-Yale as a marketing analyst, not an engineer. That is exactly
the point. The most valuable automation in a marketing team often sits
with the person who understands the process and is willing to learn
just enough technology to fix it. This project is the clearest thing I
have built at that intersection of marketing, data and engineering, and
it is the direction I want to keep moving in.

*Built with Python, the Wrike and XTM APIs, stubbornness, and quite a
few Friday nights.*
